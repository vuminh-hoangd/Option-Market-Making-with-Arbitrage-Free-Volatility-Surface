Does the second-order approximation hold?

The quotes in quoting.py come from the paper's second-order Taylor expansion (eq. 7) of the exact HJB equation (eq. 6). Footnote 5 concedes they are “not theoretically optimal” and defers the check to a preprint we do not have. This gate runs that check: pde.py solves eq. 6 by finite differences and measures the closed form against it.

01Licensing the finite-difference solution

Nothing can be measured against ground truth that has not earned the name. Three independent checks, in increasing scope.

The diffusion operator alone. Marched with no source term against two analytic answers: a linear payoff, which is both a martingale and curvature-free, must be preserved exactly (error 6.2×10−10); and E[ST2] = s2eσ2T, which genuinely exercises the second-difference term (error 1.1×10−10, halving cleanly with dt).

Two things stand out, and both cut against the expected picture.

The error saturates almost immediately. It reaches its plateau by (T−t)/T ≈ 0.25 and is flat thereafter — and twenty times the horizon moves it barely at all (1.79% → 1.87% at the baseline). The paper's “very satisfactory performance over a short market-making horizon” is true, but the horizon is not what is making it true. The error is a function of state, not of elapsed time.

It is worst where inventory is largest. At the baseline the error runs 0.23% at q = 0 and 1.79% at q = −5. That points straight at the mechanism: the expansion is taken about z = κ(δ − 1/κ) = κ(ψ1 + (2q∓1)ψ2), and its remainder grows with |z|. Both β and |q| inflate |z|; the horizon does not.

03The knife-edge question

The concern that prompted this build: β = 4357 sits exactly on √(Υβ) = α, so ζ− = 0 and ψ2 collapses to the constant −α. Was the earlier objective result an artifact of an unusually easy case?

No — but not because the knife-edge is hard either. Accuracy degrades monotonically in β, and the knife-edge is simply the middle of that trend: β/2 is more favorable to the approximation, β×2 is less. Proximity to ζ− = 0 is not the variable that matters. β is — because β sets |ψ2|, which sets |z|.

So the knife-edge was never special. It did not flatter the earlier result, and it did not disguise a problem. The question was worth asking and the answer is that it was the wrong variable to worry about.

04What the approximation actually costs

Spread error is the wrong terminal metric. What matters is what the error costs in the objective the rule exists to maximise. The exact policy is read off the PDE grid and simulated directly — same fills, same hedging, same paths — against the closed form and the constant-width benchmarks.

At all three β the closed form is statistically indistinguishable from exact — every gap sits inside its own 95% confidence interval, including at β×2 where the spreads are up to 6.8% wrong. And it beats every constant-width rival by 115 to 956.

The reason is the envelope theorem, not luck. The objective is stationary at its optimum, so a first-order error in the control produces only a second-order loss in value. A quoting rule can be several percent off in spread and still capture essentially all of the available objective.

The earlier Finding 3 stands as written. It does not need rerunning or reframing: the closed form's advantage over constant-width quoting was not an artifact of the knife-edge, and the approximation was never what was in question.

05How far to trust the closed form

  • For quoting, trust it. Within a few percent of exact on spreads and within noise on the objective, across every β and horizon tested.
  • Watch β, not the horizon. Error scales with β and with |q|, and is flat in T. A desk running a much larger inventory penalty than tested here should re-run this gate before trusting the spreads at large |q|.
  • Spread error is not objective error. Do not size a tolerance on spread accuracy alone — the two differ by an order of magnitude, in the approximation's favour.

Caveats

Single option, constant σ, one frozen Deribit snapshot (BTC, K = 80000, τ ≈ 31d, σimp = 40.75%), and one liquidity parameterisation. The coupling terms are handled explicitly, so the scheme carries a real stability limit — the reaction Jacobian grows like exp(κ|ψ2|·2qmax), which is why qmax stays at 15–20 rather than being made generously large. Tested β spans one octave either side of baseline; nothing here speaks to β orders of magnitude away, nor to the multi-option case, where the grid gains one inventory axis per instrument and this approach stops being viable.
